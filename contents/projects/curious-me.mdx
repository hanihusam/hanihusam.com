_The live product is currently down; links are for reference. I worked on it as
a full-time contract for ~3 years._

Curious Me was an anonymous social network built around location instead of followers. Share a thought, see what people nearby are saying, and join a conversation without building a public identity.

I spent three years leading frontend development across the app, public web experience, and admin tools. Working with design, I built components and interaction behavior, guided the frontend team, and owned reviews and releases. Here are a few details I particularly enjoyed working through.

## Joining an anonymous conversation

Anonymity made something as familiar as mentioning another person a little different.

The original poster appeared as **Author** in their own comment thread. Other participants received random two-word aliases. Mention suggestions only included people already in the conversation; the author didn’t need to appear because a comment already notified them.

_The recordings use the application’s UI in Storybook with mocked data. I revisited these flows for this case study; the later refinements are described below._

Those identity rules carried through posts, replies, reposts, and pinned comments. Shared components handled the common structure, while each context needed its own presentation. Connecting lines helped people follow replies. Removed comments kept their place so the surrounding conversation still made sense.

Revisiting the composer, I refined the moment between typing `@` and receiving suggestions. The loading treatment follows the suggestion list’s spacing, so the picker feels like one surface as its contents change. I also refined the feedback around publishing and revealing the new comment.

Underneath it, mentions remained part of the Draft.js content format shared by web, mobile, and backend. Improving the editor meant preserving that contract as well as making the interaction feel right.

## Keeping search steady

Search could return posts, hashtags, and rooms. Grouping them by type let people scan for the destination they wanted before reading every result.

The original implementation already handled debounced requests, grouped suggestions, full results, and empty states. But when I revisited the flow, an awkward sequence stood out: the panel briefly offered to search for the query, switched to “Please wait,” then showed the results.

Three understandable states. One unsettled interaction.

<CloudinaryVideo cloudinaryId="bapak2.dev/works/curious-me/Curious_Me_-_Search_m43oa8" />

_Searching for “Jakarta,” moving through grouped results with the keyboard, and closing the panel._

I replaced that sequence with one loading treatment aligned to the result list. The query stayed visible, and the panel held its structure while the results arrived.

Keyboard behavior needed the same attention. Arrow Up and Arrow Down now move through results with a visible active item. Enter selects once. Escape closes the panel. Clear removes the query while keeping the search interaction available.

I liked working on this because the improvement came from deciding what should stay still. Once the panel stopped repeatedly changing its presentation, the whole sequence became easier to follow.

## Opening media and finding your way back

A post card was a large navigation target. It also contained links, buttons, selectable text, and media.

That created some surprisingly easy mistakes. Opening a gallery could also open the post. Selecting text to copy could take you to another page. I fixed those boundaries across the content controls so each action kept its own intent.

<CloudinaryVideo cloudinaryId="bapak2.dev/works/curious-me/Curious_Me_-_Lightbox_Desktop_dl6myb" />

_Opening a gallery from a post, moving between images, and returning._

The media system supported images, galleries, GIFs, video, and audio. Images used aspect-ratio handling and blurred placeholders; playback responded to viewport visibility, with position preserved between related surfaces.

For this retrospective, I refined the lightbox’s framing, controls, and transitions across desktop, tablet, and mobile.

<CloudinaryVideo cloudinaryId="bapak2.dev/works/curious-me/Curious_Me_-_Lightbox_Mobile_lufpau" />

_The same gallery in its mobile presentation._

On desktop, there was room to retain more surrounding context. On mobile, the media needed more of the screen, and the controls needed to remain comfortable to reach. Checking both views exposed details that were easy to miss when working at one window size.

I tuned opening and dismissal together, including reduced-motion handling for the new transitions. Returning to the post deserved the same attention as entering the gallery.

## A post made for sharing

Curious Me also let people export a post as an image.

Pointing `html2canvas` at the existing card seemed like the obvious approach. But that card carried interactive controls, responsive styles, and application state. Each variation gave the export another way to break.

I built a second render tree whose only job was to become an image. It handled posts, comments, reposts, audio, galleries, and sensitive-content states with predictable markup. React mounted it offscreen through a portal before `html2canvas` rendered the PNG.

![Examples of Curious post cards exported as images](https://res.cloudinary.com/hanihusam/image/upload/v1783379625/bapak2.dev/works/curious-me/Screenshot_2026-07-07_at_06.12.48_rcguhv.jpg)

_One export system, several content shapes._

Then came the part that looked boring until it failed: downloading the image on a phone.

The download attribute could fail silently in mobile browsers. No file, no error. Since sharing from a phone was the point, I hardened that path by attaching the download element to the DOM, triggering it on the next animation frame, and delaying cleanup.

Giving the export its own representation made both sides easier to maintain. The live card could keep evolving without dragging every interaction detail into the image.

## What stayed with me

I would bring keyboard behavior, focus, loading continuity, and reduced motion into the conversation earlier today. Revisiting these flows showed how much they contribute to the experience.

Curious Me gave me practice moving between product rules and implementation details: deciding who belongs in a mention list, preserving that mention across platforms, or making sure opening an image doesn’t accidentally navigate the feed.

That is the work I enjoy most: following an interaction far enough to catch the details people encounter after the first click.
